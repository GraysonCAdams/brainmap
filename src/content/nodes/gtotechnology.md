## Problem

I kept learning things that took me hours to figure out and minutes to explain. In December 2008, spun off from a personal account I had started the year before, the channel existed because, in the description I wrote at the time, "many people were interested in tutorials and needed help with their computer."

## Constraints

I was twelve, so everything had to be made with software I already had, hosted somewhere free, and produced between school days.

## Approach

Follow whatever I was actually learning and teach it back. That produced an unusually incoherent catalog on paper, Windows and Mac how-tos, Photoshop, Xcode iPhone apps, a Bukkit plugin-development curriculum, Xbox hacks, and eventually a Home Assistant series, but a coherent one in practice: it is a fourteen-year record of what held my attention.

A companion site carried registration-gated downloads and forums, which mattered more than it sounds. The downloads were things I had made rather than files from the videos: a Movie Maker export preset for YouTube, a chroma-key effect, channel-layout templates. Tooling for other creators.

## Edge cases considered

Release cadence was a real decision, not a default. Once a tutorial series was finished I held the whole thing and published it as a batch, telling viewers directly that I did not want them waiting between episodes of something already done. Optimizing for the person starting the series later beat optimizing for the subscriber notification today.

When Bukkit rewrote its event system, I covered the breaking change as its own episode rather than quietly re-recording the affected tutorials. My audience was building against that API and needed the migration, not a corrected artifact.

## Tradeoffs

Chasing my own curiosity meant the channel never had a niche, and it flatlined for roughly seven years because of it. The counter-evidence is that my two biggest videos were both accidents, a clip about streaming Spotify to an Xbox 360 and a two-minute clip about escaping a room in Skyrim. Utility content outperformed every deliberate series I ever planned.

## Outcome

**7.92K subscribers, 1.24M lifetime views.** The arc is 588 subscribers in 2011, 2,248 by the end of 2016, then dormant, then roughly quadrupled by a 2020-21 Home Assistant series that landed while I was deep in my own homelab. The channel doubled as the growth funnel for AdventureLobbies for several years, with the product's name literally in the banner.

By 2021 I had renamed it to my own and rewritten the mission as "teaching others how to accomplish creative tech projects while understanding every step along the way," which is close enough to why this site exists.
