## Problem

By 2021 I had a full-time SRE job and was taking on one last large freelance engagement, a property investment firm. The risk was not the work. It was that the work would expand quietly, one reasonable request at a time, until it consumed every weekend for a year.

## Constraints

I had maybe a few hours a week. The client, correctly, did not think in deliverables; they thought in outcomes, and would describe those in notes like "get head shots, waiting for company" and a page reference in all caps. Turning that into a bounded list was my job, not theirs.

## Approach

I split scope out of the contract into a separate document and had both e-signed the same day. The agreement covered money and ownership. The checklist covered exactly what would exist at the end, itemized, and it was a signed artifact in its own right rather than an email attachment.

Then I tracked it on a shared project board, one task per line item, so the client could see the same list I was working from.

## Edge cases considered

The checklist has to be signed separately, not folded into the contract, or it stops functioning. Buried as an appendix, "that's not on the list" reads as lawyering. As its own thing both parties signed on the same afternoon, it reads as the plan we agreed on, and pointing at it is a normal conversation rather than an escalation.

One line item on it is "install website platform (WordPress recommended)". Seven years earlier my resume had bragged about building sites without platforms. Here the platform is not just accepted, it is a contractual deliverable that the client signed off on, alongside a sixty-two dollar receipt for the theme. I could not have written that sentence at nineteen without embarrassment. At twenty-six it was the honest description of what serves the client.

Hosting went on the client's own billing details, so the recurring cost was visible to them from day one and did not depend on me forwarding an invoice.

## Tradeoffs

Writing the checklist took real hours before any billable work began, on the largest job I had taken, at a lower rate than I had charged three years earlier. I also swapped my old initiation fee for a non-refundable deposit, which is worse for me if the job runs long and better for me if it never starts.

All three of those choices trade money for predictability. That was the right trade for someone with a day job and no capacity for a project that would not end.

## Outcome

Delivered inside its window and it was the last big engagement I took. The general lesson has outlived the freelance practice entirely: the artifact that prevents scope creep is not a clause in a contract, it is a list both people signed and can point at without it feeling like an accusation.
