## Problem

A legal nurse consulting practice needed a website its owner could keep current without calling anyone. In 2011 the realistic options for a small practice were a static site that ossifies the moment the developer leaves, or a platform the owner can actually operate.

## Constraints

I was fifteen. That cuts in a less obvious direction than it sounds: I had no reputation to trade on, so nothing about the engagement could rest on the client believing I knew what I was doing. It had to be checkable. It also meant whatever I built had to survive me going to school, then college, then a full-time job.

## Approach

WordPress, on a theme shared with the practice's sister site, so two properties shared one set of decisions instead of becoming two maintenance problems. The design was not the point. The owner being able to publish without me was the point.

I put my name in the footer.

## Edge cases considered

The footer credit was not only vanity, though at fifteen it was partly that. It was the only marketing channel available to someone with no portfolio, no references, and no plausible way to cold-pitch a business. A credit on a live site is a claim anyone can verify by looking, which is the exact opposite of a teenager's assurance that he can build a website.

It also turned out to be the only durable record. The source is long gone, my memory of the year was wrong, and the sole reason this engagement can be dated is that the Internet Archive captured the site in February 2011 with no credit and again that November with one. A nine month bracket, from evidence rather than recollection, which is more than I can say for anything I remembered about it.

Sharing a theme with the sister property was a bet that the two would stay similar. It paid: years later their content converged and one domain now redirects to the other, and that merge was cheap precisely because they had never really diverged.

## Tradeoffs

A shared theme across two client sites means every change to one has to be weighed against the other, with no version control, no staging and no tests. That was ordinary practice for small WordPress work at the time and it was still fragile. Every edit went straight to production, because production was the only environment that existed.

Handing the owner publishing rights also hands them the ability to break the layout, and occasionally they did. I decided early that a site the owner can damage and repair beats one they cannot touch, because the alternative makes me a permanent dependency for changing a phone number.

## Outcome

Live continuously since 2011, and I still maintain it. The practice absorbed its sister site along the way, and this is the domain that survived.

The lesson I would hand back to the fifteen year old is the thing he accidentally got right. Put your name on the work. A credit on something running in public is the only credential that does not require anyone to take your word for it, and it stays legible long after you have forgotten the details yourself.
